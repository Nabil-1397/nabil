**Claim labels.**
- **[FACT]**: verifiable event, text, date or statistic.
- **[INTERP: whose]**: an interpretation, with the scholar or panellist who holds it.
- **[NORM]**: a normative argument about what *ought* to be done.

**Evidence grades** for key conclusions: **Strong** (multiple independent sources, little scholarly dispute), **Moderate** (good evidence, live dispute), **Weak** (plausible, thinly evidenced or speculative).

**Citations.** Only real works are cited. Where a page number, edition or exact figure could not be confirmed from memory, it is marked **[VERIFY]**. Statistical figures are rounded and should be refreshed from the World Bank *World Development Indicators* (WDI), UNDP *Human Development Report* (HDR) and Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) before any public use. Facts about events after mid-2025 are marked [VERIFY] because they move fast.

**Two corrections to the brief, up front.**
1. Richard Eaton's book is *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* (University of California Press, 1993), not "Islam on the Bengal frontier".
2. The 1940 Lahore Resolution's plural "Independent States" is accurate, but it was amended to the singular "a sovereign independent State" at the Delhi Muslim League Legislators' Convention of April 1946. Both texts matter (see Phase 5).

## PHASE 1: Genealogy of the concept "civilizational state"

### 1.1 Overview

**[INTERP: PT]** The term has three lives. (1) A **descriptive-sinological** life (Pye, Tu): China is "a civilization pretending to be a state", meaning the modern Westphalian state is an awkward container for a much older cultural order. (2) A **legitimating** life (Jacques, Zhang): the civilizational state is a superior or at least equal alternative to the Western nation-state, and justifies a distinct political model. (3) A **diagnostic-critical** life (Coker, Rachman, Maçães, Acharya, Therborn): the term is now a rhetorical weapon used by Beijing, Moscow, New Delhi, Ankara and others to reject liberal universalism and to justify strong leaders, minority subordination and spheres of influence.

**Evidence grade for this three-phase reading: Moderate.** The periodisation is widely used in the secondary literature, but the boundaries between phases are blurry (Jacques is also partly descriptive).

### 1.3 Panel debate on Phase 1

- **PT:** "The concept is a family of claims, not one claim. Separate (a) *continuity* (a long cultural history exists), (b) *exceptionalism* (therefore universal norms don't apply to us), and (c) *hierarchy* (therefore the core culture has a privileged position in the state). Bangladesh can accept (a) without (b) and (c)."
- **CP:** "In practice, (a) almost never stays alone. In every declared civilizational state I study, the language arrives with (b) and (c) within a decade."
- **IT:** "That is an empirical generalisation from five cases with powerful centralising states. It does not establish necessity."
- **HB:** "Most of these authors treat civilizations as *continuous*. Bengal's story is one of *synthesis and rupture*. If you import the Chinese template, you will force a false continuity on Bengal."

**Conclusion 1.** The concept moved from diagnosis (Pye) to self-legitimation (Zhang) to critique (Acharya). Its scholarly core is useful only if continuity claims are separated from exceptionalism and hierarchy. **Evidence grade: Strong** on the genealogy; **Moderate** on CP's claim that continuity talk tends to slide into hierarchy.

## PHASE 2: Definitions and distinctions

### 2.1 Comparison table

| Dimension | **Civilizational state** | **Nation-state** | **State-nation** (Stepan, Linz and Yadav) | **Empire** | **Ummah-based polity** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Source of legitimacy** | Guardianship of a long civilization; continuity, cultural mission; performance (often) | Popular sovereignty of one "nation"; self-determination | Constitutional patriotism plus recognition of multiple cultural identities; democratic consent | Dynastic or imperial right; conquest; civilising mission; order | Sovereignty of God (*hakimiyya*) as mediated by shari'a; ruler as trustee (*amana*); consultation (*shura*) and allegiance (*bay'a*) |
| **Boundary of membership** | Cultural belonging; often fuzzy and transborder (diaspora, "Russian world", "cultural China") | Citizenship, ideally coextensive with the nation | Legal citizenship; nested, multiple identities expected ("I can be Tamil and Indian") | Subject status, graded by rank, religion, region | Faith (Muslims as full members); non-Muslims as protected communities (*dhimmis*, historically), or in modern reinterpretation as equal citizens |
| **Role of religion** | Varies: Confucian ethics (China), Orthodoxy (Russia), Hindu culture (India), Sunni-Turkish synthesis (Turkey), Shi'i jurisprudence (Iran) | Varies from laic (France) to established church (Denmark); religion usually subordinate to the nation | Accommodated plurally; "respect and support" (state may fund multiple religions; Stepan's "twin tolerations") | Instrumental; usually tolerant if loyal (millet system, Mughal *sulh-i kull*) | Constitutive: the polity exists to uphold the faith and the law |
| **Role of language** | One civilizational language privileged (Mandarin, Russian, Hindi, Turkish, Persian) | One national language central to identity | Multiple official languages possible (India's Eighth Schedule; Belgium; Switzerland) | Court/administrative language plus many local languages | Arabic as sacred language; vernaculars tolerated or celebrated |
| **Treatment of minorities** | Tolerated within a hierarchy; subject to "integration"; dissenters frame as "alien" | Assimilation or minority rights regimes; historically prone to homogenisation and expulsion | Territorial and cultural autonomy; asymmetric federalism | Differentiated rights; autonomy in exchange for loyalty and tax | Classical: *dhimma* (protection plus jizya, some disabilities). Modern reformist: equal citizenship (*muwatana*) |
| **Relation to international order** | Revisionist toward liberal universalism; spheres of influence; "multipolarity" | Westphalian sovereignty; UN system | Westphalian; often multilateralist | Hierarchical; suzerainty, tribute | Theoretically transnational (ummah over borders); in practice OIC-style cooperation among sovereign states |

**[FACT]** The state-nation concept is set out in Alfred Stepan, Juan J. Linz and Yogendra Yadav, *Crafting State-Nations: India and Other Multinational Democracies* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2011). Their "twin tolerations" (religious authorities tolerate democratic institutions, and the state tolerates religious citizens' public participation) come from Stepan, "Religion, Democracy, and the 'Twin Tolerations'", *Journal of Democracy* 11(4), 2000, pp. 37–57.

**[INTERP: CL]** The most realistic legal home for Bangladesh is a hybrid: a *nation-state* in its core legal form (single citizenship, single sovereignty) with *state-nation* safeguards for the Chittagong Hill Tracts (CHT) and religious minorities. "Civilizational" talk would then be a cultural and educational layer, not a legal hierarchy.

**[INTERP: IT]** Disagrees: "If you strip all legal content from the civilizational idea, you have only rhetoric. Islam has legal content. The honest question is *how much* law and *for whom*, not whether."

### 2.2 Working definition

**[NORM, proposed by PT and CL with dissent noted]**

> A **civilizational state** is a sovereign state whose political community understands itself, and organises some public institutions, as the present custodian of a historically deep, distinctive and still-living cultural synthesis that is larger in time (and sometimes in space) than the modern state itself, and which claims that this synthesis supplies norms for public life that are not fully reducible to universal liberal or market principles.

### 2.3 Testable criteria (7)

Each criterion can be scored 0 (absent), 1 (partial), 2 (strong) using evidence.

| # | Criterion | How to test | Key evidence type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** | **Historical depth**: a recognisable cultural synthesis persisting for at least several centuries before the modern state | Continuous literary, architectural, legal, religious traditions; recognition by outside observers | Manuscripts, monuments, inscriptions, coinage, travellers' accounts |
| **C2** | **Distinctiveness**: the synthesis is meaningfully different from its neighbours, not just a regional variant | Comparative analysis with neighbouring traditions (North Indian Islam, West Bengal Hindu culture, Arakan, Assam) | Literary and religious comparison; linguistic data |
| **C3** | **Mass, living continuity**: the synthesis is lived by ordinary people now, not only by elites or museums | Survey data on identity; ritual practice; popular culture | World Values Survey, Pew; ethnography |
| **C4** | **Institutional translation**: the state actually organises public institutions (education, law, cultural policy) around the synthesis | Constitution, curricula, state cultural budgets, courts | Legal texts, budgets, syllabi |
| **C5** | **Normative self-understanding**: elites and citizens claim the synthesis supplies public norms beyond liberal or market principles | Party manifestos, intellectual production, public discourse | Discourse analysis |
| **C6** | **Inclusive capacity**: the synthesis can include citizens who do not share its religious core without legal subordination | Equal rights in law and practice; minority representation; violence data | Court records, HR reports, minority survey data |
| **C7** | **External recognition and projection**: other states or civilizations recognise the polity as a cultural pole; the state projects the synthesis abroad | Diplomacy, diaspora institutions, cultural exports, scholarship | Foreign policy documents, soft-power indices |

**Dissent.**
- **CP:** "C6 is a normative criterion smuggled into a descriptive definition. China and Iran score low on C6 but are clearly civilizational states." **PT's reply:** "Agreed. So split them: C1–C5 and C7 define *whether* a state is civilizational; C6 measures *whether it is a good one*. We keep C6 because the study circle wants a framework that survives its critics."
- **HB:** "C1 will be gamed. Every nationalist claims depth. Insist on primary-source evidence, not slogans."

**Conclusion 2.** A definition with explicit, scorable criteria is the only way to stop the concept being used as a slogan. **Evidence grade: Moderate** (definitional proposals cannot be "proven", but the criteria are operational).

## PHASE 3: Islamic intellectual resources

### 3.1 Scriptural and early anchors (for orientation)

- **[FACT]** Qur'an 49:13: "O mankind, We created you from a male and female and made you peoples (*shu'uban*) and tribes (*qaba'il*) that you may know one another (*li-ta'arafu*). The most noble of you in the sight of God is the most righteous." Frequently cited as recognising peoplehood while denying it rank.
- **[FACT]** Qur'an 30:22 lists "the diversity of your tongues and colours" among God's signs. Cited by Bengali Muslim writers to sanctify the mother tongue.
- **[FACT]** A hadith in Sunan Abi Dawud (Book of Etiquette, no. 5121 in common numbering [VERIFY]): "He is not of us who calls to *'asabiyya*, who fights for *'asabiyya*, or who dies for *'asabiyya*." Central to Islamist critiques of nationalism. Another report defines *'asabiyya* as "helping your people in wrongdoing" (Abu Dawud 5119 [VERIFY]), which reformists cite to argue that only *unjust* partisanship is condemned.
- **[FACT]** The "Constitution of Medina" (*Sahifat al-Madina*) describes the believers and certain Jewish tribes as "one community (*umma wahida*) apart from other people" in some clauses, while preserving their separate religions. **[INTERP: IT]** Reformists (e.g., An-Na'im's circle, and many Indonesian scholars) read this as a precedent for a plural political community; others read the Jewish clauses as alliance, not common citizenship. For text and analysis: Michael Lecker, *The "Constitution of Medina": Muhammad's First Legal Document* (Darwin Press, 2004).

### 3.3 Is "Muslim civilisational nationalism" coherent, or a contradiction between *ummah* and *qawm*?

**Panel debate.**

- **IT (position A, contradiction):** "On the Maududian and many Salafi readings, yes: *qawmiyya* (nationalism) is the modern form of *'asabiyya*, and loyalty to a territorial nation over the global ummah is *jahiliyya*. A Muslim *nationalism* is a contradiction; a Muslim *civilization* is not, but it is not national."
- **IT (position B, layered coherence):** "On the Iqbal–Madani axis, no: the ummah is a moral-religious community, the qawm a political one. Iqbal's 'League of Nations' model allows national units. Qur'an 49:13 recognises *shu'ub*. Every Muslim polity in history was a particular polity (Abbasid, Mughal, Bengal Sultanate)."
- **PT:** "There's a hidden third option. 'Civilisational' does work 'national' cannot: it names a *synthesis*, not a race or a religion. 'Bengal-delta Islamic civilisation' is a regional civilization within the larger Islamicate world (Marshall Hodgson's term in *The Venture of Islam*, 3 vols, University of Chicago Press, 1974). Persianate Iran, Ottoman Anatolia, Malay Nusantara and Bengal are parallel regional Islamicate civilizations."
- **HB:** "Historically, Bengal Muslims lived *both* identities without a sense of contradiction: Arabic-script piety plus Bengali-language devotional literature. The contradiction arises only when the state demands *exclusive* primacy for one level."
- **CL:** "Coherence depends on who is left out. If 'Muslim' qualifies 'civilisational' in law, non-Muslim citizens become second-class. If it qualifies it as *historical description* (the delta civilization was shaped decisively by Islam), it is coherent and compatible with equal citizenship."

**Panel resolution (with dissent).**

> **[NORM, majority: PT, HB, CL, IT-position B, DE]** "Bangla Muslim civilisational nationalism" is coherent **if and only if** (1) the *ummah* is treated as a moral-religious horizon, not a rival political sovereign; (2) the *qawm* is defined civically and territorially (all citizens of Bangladesh), with the *civilizational* content describing the historically dominant synthesis, not a criterion of membership; and (3) the word "Muslim" describes the synthesis's formative influence, not a legal rank among citizens.
>
> **[NORM, dissent: IT-position A]** Without a commitment to shari'a as public law, the phrase is "Islam as folklore", using religion for nationalist ends.
>
> **[INTERP, dissent: CP]** Whatever theorists intend, in practice the qualifier "Muslim" will be read by voters and activists as a membership test.

**Evidence grade: Moderate.** The textual resources for a layered reading are real (Iqbal, Madani, Qur'an 49:13, Hodgson's "Islamicate"), but so is a long, authoritative tradition (Maududi, Qutb, many 'ulama') that rejects nationalism. The question is not settled in Islamic thought; it is a live choice.

### 5.2 Eaton's "Bengal frontier" thesis

**[FACT]** Richard M. Eaton, *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* (University of California Press, 1993). Key elements:

1. **The puzzle.** The 1872 census showed a Muslim *majority* in eastern Bengal, far from the north Indian centres of Muslim power, which surprised British officials (H. Beverley's census report).
2. **Rejected theories (ch. 5).** Eaton rejects (a) conversion by the sword (no evidence; the most Islamised zone was furthest from the capitals), (b) immigration (cannot account for the numbers), (c) patronage (conversion strongest where state presence was weakest), and (d) "religion of social liberation" from caste (east Bengal populations were not well integrated into Brahmanical caste society in the first place).
3. **His thesis.** As the active delta shifted east (rivers changed course in the 16th century), Mughal-era grants of forest land were given to pioneers, often *pirs* and mosque-builders, who led communities in clearing jungle and growing wet rice. Islam spread as a religion of the agrarian frontier, associated with civilisation-building itself (clearing forest, founding villages, cultivating rice).
4. **Gradual process.** Conversion was slow: *inclusion* (Allah and Islamic figures added to local pantheons), *identification* (Islamic names merged with local deities), *displacement* (exclusive Islamic identity), with the last stage largely in the 19th–20th centuries under reformist movements.

**[INTERP: panel]**
- **HB:** "Eaton's thesis is the single most important scholarly resource for a Bangladeshi civilizational narrative: Islam in the delta is *indigenous*, a peasant civilisational project, not a foreign implant. Grade: **Strong** as the leading scholarly account, though it is debated."
- **IT:** "It also cuts against Islamist purism: the 'pure' Islam of reform movements came *late*; for most of the history, Bengali Islam was syncretic. So which Islam is the civilization built on?"
- **HB (critics of Eaton):** Some scholars argue the frontier thesis overstates ecology and understates Sufi networks, earlier pre-Mughal conversion and coastal trade (Arab and Persian merchant links to Chittagong). See critiques in reviews and later work; e.g., Asim Roy's emphasis on cultural mediation [VERIFY specific critical reviews]. Grade on the full thesis: **Moderate**.

### 5.8 Strongest case that Bangladesh IS a distinct civilizational unit

**[NORM/INTERP: argued by HB-majority and IT, as a steelman]**

1. **A distinct ecology produced a distinct civilization.** The active Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta, with its shifting rivers, wet-rice frontier and monsoon, shaped a peasant society unlike the Indo-Gangetic plain. Eaton shows Islam became the religion of this specific civilising process. That makes Bengali Islam *indigenous*, not imported, and distinct from both North Indian Islam and Hindu Bengal.
2. **Two centuries of independent statehood.** The Bengal Sultanate (1352–1576) was a recognised sovereign with its own architecture, coinage, diplomacy (Ming China, Timurid and Arab worlds) and literary patronage that elevated Bengali.
3. **Continuous vernacular-Islamic literature.** From *Yusuf-Zulekha* through Alaol, Abdul Hakim, the *puthi* literature, Nazrul and Jasimuddin, a body of Bengali-language Muslim literature exists that has no exact parallel elsewhere.
4. **Repeated self-assertion against both larger neighbours.** 1905–11 (against Calcutta-centred Hindu elite opposition to an East Bengal province), 1940 (Lahore "States"), 1947 (a Muslim-majority eastern state), 1952 and 1971 (against Pakistani-Urdu domination), 2024 (against a regime seen as dependent on India). The delta's people have repeatedly refused absorption into both Indian and Pakistani projects.
5. **Demographic and linguistic weight.** Some 170+ million people, about 91% Muslim and over 98% Bengali-speaking (BBS Census 2022 [VERIFY language figure]), make Bangladesh the world's largest Bengali-speaking polity and the largest Muslim-majority population with a non-Arabic, non-Persian, non-Urdu literary tradition outside Indonesia.
6. **Global contribution.** The Language Movement gave the world International Mother Language Day; Bangladesh gave the world large-scale microfinance and NGO-led development (Grameen, BRAC).

**Grade: Moderate.** Each premise is factually defensible; the conclusion ("civilizational unit" rather than "regional variant") is interpretive.

### 5.9 Strongest case that Bangladesh is NOT a distinct civilizational unit

**[NORM/INTERP: argued by HB-minority, PT, CP]**

1. **It is a region, not a civilization.** Bengal is a region within two larger civilizations: the Indic (language, music, food, kinship, caste remnants) and the Islamicate (religion, law, Persian loanwords). Calling a region a civilization inflates the category; by the same standard Punjab, Kerala and Sindh would be civilizations.
2. **The Bengali half of the synthesis is shared with West Bengal.** Rabindranath Tagore, Bankim, Jibanananda, the Bengali script, the national anthem and the 1952 cause belong to a language community split by a 1947 border. A "Bangladeshi civilization" must either claim Tagore (and then it is not distinct from West Bengal) or disown him (and then it is impoverished).
3. **The Muslim half is shared with 1.9 billion people.** Its distinctiveness comes from syncretism, which modern reformist Islam has spent two centuries removing.
4. **Discontinuity, not continuity.** Mass Islamisation happened mainly in the 16th–19th centuries; the Sultanate elite was foreign; the modern identity was produced by the 19th-century census, reform movements and 20th-century politics. Civilizational "continuity" back to the Palas is a retrospective construction.
5. **The state's founding is a rejection of religion-based nationhood.** 1971 was fought *against* a state whose legitimacy was Muslim nationhood. A Muslim civilizational state re-imports the logic of the state Bangladeshis rejected.
6. **No external recognition.** Neither Huntington, nor Therborn, nor any major comparative scholar lists Bangladesh as a civilizational state.

**Grade: Moderate.** Points 1, 2 and 5 are the hardest for the framework to answer.

### 5.10 Panel verdict on Phase 5

> **[INTERP, majority]** Bangladesh is best described as the **political core of a distinct regional Islamicate civilization of the Bengal delta**, sharing its language with a larger Bengali world and its faith with the global ummah. It is a civilizational *society* with deep roots; whether it becomes a civilizational *state* is a political choice, not a historical fact. **Grade: Moderate.**
>
> **[Dissent, PT]** "Regional Islamicate civilization" is analytically fine. Adding "nationalism" to it is where danger starts.
>
> **[Dissent, IT]** Recognising Bangladesh's Islamic civilizational depth is not dangerous; *denying* it, as 1972-style secularism tried, produced a backlash that strengthened hardline Islamism.

## PHASE 6: Steelmanning the critics

### (a) Secular Bengali nationalism

**Steelman, in its own terms.**
> "We did not shed blood in 1971 to replace a Pakistani Muslim nation with a Bangladeshi Muslim nation. The Pakistan project told us that Islam made us one people with Punjabis; then it killed our students for speaking Bengali and slaughtered us in 1971 with the help of collaborators (Razakars, Al-Badr) who claimed to defend Islam. The Language Movement and the Liberation War founded a nation on *language and culture shared by Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists and Christians*. The 1972 Constitution's secularism (Art. 12) did not mean hostility to religion; it meant ending communalism, state patronage of any one religion and the political use of religion. Every time the state has re-Islamised (1977, 1988), it has been under military rule. 'Muslim civilisational nationalism' is the two-nation theory with a new label."

Key texts: 1972 Constitution, Arts. 8, 9, 12; Tajuddin Ahmad's and Sheikh Mujib's speeches; Anisuzzaman, *Muslim Manas o Bangla Sahitya* (1964) [VERIFY title transliteration]; Ahmed Sharif's essays.

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** that 1971 was, at minimum, a rejection of *religion as sufficient ground for nationhood* and of *religion as an instrument of ethnic domination*.
2. **Build in** the Language Movement and Liberation War as foundational, not subordinate, events, including honest recognition of the role of Islamist collaborators in 1971.
3. **Build in** constitutional non-establishment safeguards (no state privileging of one faith in rights) even if Islam's cultural primacy is recognised.

### (b) Liberal and human-rights critiques

**Steelman.**
> "Civilizational talk replaces citizens with cultural types. Rights belong to persons, not civilizations. Every declared civilizational state in Phase 4 has used the concept to exempt itself from universal standards ('Western values'), to delegitimise dissent ('foreign agent', 'anti-national'), and to rank citizens. The burden of proof lies on those who would add a civilizational qualifier to a constitution that already promises equality (Art. 27) and non-discrimination (Art. 28). Bangladesh's own record (enforced disappearances documented by the Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances, 2024–25 [VERIFY], the Digital Security Act 2018, attacks on Hindus in Ramu 2012, Comilla 2021) shows that the problem is too little liberty, not too little civilization."

Key texts: John Rawls, *Political Liberalism* (Columbia University Press, 1993); Amartya Sen, *Identity and Violence: The Illusion of Destiny* (W. W. Norton, 2006), especially his critique of "solitarist" identity and civilizational partitioning; ICCPR (Bangladesh acceded 2000).

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** that universal human rights bind Bangladesh (treaty obligations) and that civilizational identity cannot justify derogation.
2. **Build in** a "no-ranking" rule: no legal right, office, or benefit may depend on religion or ethnicity. Amartya Sen, a Bengali, is the obvious interlocutor: his argument that identities are plural and chosen should be engaged, not ignored.
3. **Build in** independent institutions (judiciary, human rights commission, election commission) *before* expanding cultural-identity policy.

### (c) Islamist universalism: nationalism as *jahili 'asabiyya*

**Steelman.**
> "The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: he is not of us who calls to *'asabiyya*. The ummah is one body; the borders of Bangladesh were drawn by Cyril Radcliffe in 1947. Nationalism (*qawmiyya, wataniyya*) is a Western import that broke the Ottoman caliphate and divided Muslims into 57 states that cannot defend Gaza or the Rohingya. A 'Muslim civilisational nationalism' uses Islam as a decoration for the same idolatry of the nation (*watan-parasti*) that Iqbal condemned. Either God's law is sovereign, or the nation is; you cannot serve two masters. A Bengali-Muslim identity that celebrates Pohela Boishakh's *mangal shobhajatra* and Baul syncretism alongside Islam dilutes *tawhid*."

Key texts: Maududi on nationalism (see Phase 3); Sayyid Qutb, *Ma'alim fi al-Tariq* (*Milestones*, 1964); Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani (Hizb ut-Tahrir); Abu Dawud hadith on *'asabiyya*.

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** that the ummah is a real moral community and that the framework must not make the nation an object of worship or an absolute.
2. **Build in** foreign-policy commitments that give the ummah concrete content (Rohingya protection, Palestine, OIC engagement), so the national frame serves rather than replaces Muslim solidarity.
3. **Build in** respect for the *'ulama'* and madrasa sector as part of the civilization, including Qawmi madrasa recognition (the Dawra-e-Hadith degree was recognised as equivalent to a master's in 2018 [FACT]).
4. **Answer** (not concede): the hadith condemns *unjust* partisanship; Qur'an 49:13 recognises peoples; the Islamic tradition always had particular polities. The framework should state clearly that it does **not** claim to be an Islamic state in the Maududian sense.

### (d) Marxist and class critiques (e.g., Badruddin Umar)

**Steelman.**
> "Every identity project in Bengal has been driven by a class. In 1905 and 1947, the Muslim *jotedar* and emerging middle class used religion against Hindu landlords and moneylenders; in 1952 and 1971 the Bengali petty bourgeoisie used language against the West Pakistani bourgeoisie; after 1975 the military-bureaucratic elite and the new garment and trading bourgeoisie used Islam for legitimacy. 'Civilisational nationalism' is the newest ideology of a comprador and rent-seeking ruling class: it gives garment workers (mostly young women paid among the lowest wages in the world), landless peasants and migrant workers pride instead of wages. Rana Plaza (24 April 2013, 1,134 dead) is the true face of the 'civilization'."

Key texts: Badruddin Umar, *Purba Banglar Bhasha Andolon o Tatkalin Rajniti* (3 vols, 1970–1985); *Sampradayikata* (1966) [VERIFY title and date]; *The Emergence of Bangladesh*, 2 vols (Oxford University Press, Karachi, 2004–2006); Hamza Alavi, "The State in Post-Colonial Societies: Pakistan and Bangladesh", *New Left Review* I/74 (1972), pp. 59–81.

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** that identity politics in Bengal has repeatedly been mobilised by elites for class ends.
2. **Build in** a material programme: labour rights (minimum wage review, freedom of association, occupational safety from the Accord on Fire and Building Safety model), land reform where feasible, and social protection. Islamic *zakat* and *waqf* can be framed as civilizational redistribution.
3. **Build in** measurable distributional targets (Gini, labour share, minimum wage relative to living-wage estimates) so the framework is accountable.

### (e) Hindu, Buddhist, Christian and Adivasi citizens, including the Chittagong Hill Tracts

**Steelman.**
> "We are not guests in this land; our ancestors built Paharpur and Mahasthangarh, wrote the Charyapada and composed 'Amar Sonar Bangla'. The Hindu share of the population fell from about 22% (1951) to about 8% (2022 census: 7.95%) [FACT, BBS]. The Enemy Property Act (1965), continued as the Vested Property Act, transferred huge amounts of Hindu land (Abul Barkat's research estimated millions of acres and households affected [VERIFY figures]). Every time politics turns religious, temples burn: 1990, 1992 (after Babri), 2001 post-election violence, 2012 Ramu Buddhist temples, 2021 Durga Puja attacks, August 2024. In the Chittagong Hill Tracts, state-sponsored settlement of Bengalis (1979–85), decades of militarisation, and the incomplete implementation of the 1997 Peace Accord (land commission still largely ineffective) are the reality. Our 1972 MP, M. N. Larma, warned that we are not Bengalis; the 2011 Constitution still calls the nation 'Bangalee' and calls us 'tribes, minor races, ethnic sects and communities' (Art. 23A). A 'Bangla Muslim' civilization is a civilization in which we are by definition not full members."

Key texts: CHT Peace Accord (2 Dec 1997); Chittagong Hill Tracts Commission reports; Amena Mohsin, *The Politics of Nationalism: The Case of the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Bangladesh* (University Press Limited, 1997); Bangladesh Hindu Buddhist Christian Unity Council reports [VERIFY specific reports]; Abul Barkat et al., *Deprivation of Hindu Minority in Bangladesh: Living with Vested Property* (Pathak Shamabesh, 2008) [VERIFY title].

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** the civilization of the delta is plural in *origin*, not only in present population; Buddhist, Hindu and Adivasi layers are constitutive, not tolerated additions.
2. **Rename if necessary.** A name that excludes ("Bangla *Muslim*") may need to become "Bengal-delta civilization" or "Bangladeshi civilization, shaped decisively by Islam".
3. **Build in** constitutional recognition of Indigenous/Adivasi peoples (contested term in Bangladesh; the state has resisted "Indigenous"), full implementation of the CHT Accord (land commission, withdrawal of temporary camps), repeal or full remedy of the Vested Property regime (the Vested Property Return Act 2001 and its amendments have had partial implementation), and minority representation guarantees.
4. **Build in** a rapid-response mechanism for communal violence (see Phase 7).

### (f) International-relations realists

**Steelman.**
> "States pursue power and security; 'civilization' is rhetoric that rationalises interest. China talks of civilizational harmony while building bases; Russia of the Russian world while invading; India of *vasudhaiva kutumbakam* while building fences. Bangladesh is a small-territory state surrounded on three sides by India, with China as its largest trading-goods supplier and the US and EU as its biggest export markets. Its survival depends on balance, not identity. Civilizational rhetoric will (i) alarm India, which sees 'Bangla Muslim' identity through the lens of Assam and West Bengal migration politics, (ii) be useless to Gulf states that care about labour supply and investment returns, not shared civilization, and (iii) raise expectations at home (e.g., about Rohingya or Palestine) that the state cannot meet."

Key texts: Kenneth Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (Addison-Wesley, 1979); John Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (W. W. Norton, 2001); Hans Morgenthau, *Politics Among Nations* (1948).

**What a credible framework must concede or build in.**
1. **Concede** that civilizational identity cannot substitute for hard capacity: economic strength, defence, diplomacy.
2. **Build in** a "no irredentism" and "non-interference" clause: Bangladesh makes no claim on Indian or Myanmar territory or populations.
3. **Use** identity as soft power (language, culture, diaspora, humanitarian leadership) while keeping alignment decisions interest-based.

### 7.4 Economic statecraft

**[INTERP: DE]** The civilizational frame has no economic content of its own. Its only economic value is as a *mobilising narrative* for long-horizon, disciplined development, as Japan's "rich country, strong army" (*fukoku kyohei*), Korea's Park-era nationalism and Malaysia's NEP and Vision 2020 did. Without delivery, it becomes compensation for failure.

### 7.6 Minority protections and safeguards against majoritarian drift

**[NORM, CL, endorsed by the full panel including IT]** A civilizational framework is credible only if it ships with *harder* minority protections than the status quo. Minimum package:

1. **Constitutional:** entrenched no-ranking clause; recognition of Adivasi peoples; proportional representation or reserved seats mechanism for religious and ethnic minorities in Parliament and local government [NORM; debate: reserved seats can ghettoise].
2. **Statutory:** a comprehensive **Anti-Discrimination Act** (a draft was placed before Parliament in 2022 [VERIFY]); a **Minority Protection Commission** with investigative powers; a **Vested Property** final settlement with a time-bound restitution tribunal.
3. **CHT:** time-bound implementation roadmap for the 1997 Accord, especially the Land Dispute Resolution Commission and devolution to the Regional Council and Hill District Councils.
4. **Communal violence:** a rapid-response protocol (police accountability for failure to protect; special tribunals with deadlines; compensation fund); systematic data collection by an independent body.
5. **Speech:** blasphemy-type offences (Penal Code s. 295A; the Digital Security Act 2018, replaced by the Cyber Security Act 2023, itself replaced by an ordinance in 2025 [VERIFY]) must not be used against minorities or dissenters; require high evidentiary thresholds and prosecutorial approval.
6. **Monitoring:** an annual **Pluralism Index** published by an independent body, scoring the Phase 2 criterion C6.

**[Dissent, IT-position A]** "Item 5 will be read as protecting insults to the Prophet. Any party adopting it will lose religious voters." **[Response, CL]** "Then specify: protect religious *feelings* through civil remedies and narrowly drawn incitement law, not broad criminal blasphemy, which Pakistan shows leads to vigilante killing."

## CONCLUDING MATERIALS

### (1) Executive summary (one page)

**Question.** Should Bangladesh understand itself as a civilizational state rooted in the Bengali-Muslim synthesis of the Bengal delta, and what would that mean in practice?

**1. The concept.** "Civilizational state" moved from Lucian Pye's 1990 *diagnosis* of China's dysfunction, through Zhang Weiwei's 2012 *self-legitimation* of Chinese exceptionalism, to a *critique* (Coker, Rachman, Maçães, Acharya) that sees it as a tool for strongmen, exceptionalism and minority subordination. Acharya's "myth" critique is the test any Bangladeshi version must pass. **(Strong)**

**2. Definition.** We propose seven testable criteria (depth, distinctiveness, living continuity, institutional translation, normative self-understanding, inclusive capacity, external recognition). Bangladesh scores roughly 8–10 of 14: it is a **civilizational society**, not yet a civilizational state. **(Moderate)**

**3. Islamic thought.** Islamic tradition contains both objections (Maududi: nationalism is a Western idol; Hallaq: the modern "Islamic state" is impossible) and resources (Ibn Khaldun's *'asabiyya*; Iqbal's Muslim "League of Nations"; Madani's composite nationalism; Bennabi's civilisation-as-work; An-Na'im's civic reason). "Muslim civilisational nationalism" is **coherent only** if the ummah remains a moral horizon, the nation is civic, and "Muslim" describes the synthesis's formation rather than a membership test. **(Moderate; live dispute)**

**4. Comparisons.** Civilizational rhetoric does **not** predict growth; export industrialisation, education and state capacity do. Declared civilizational states (China, India, Russia, Turkey, Iran) show a strong pattern of minority subordination and executive aggrandisement. The best Muslim-majority precedents are **Indonesia's Pancasila** (civic-theistic pluralism) and **Malaysia's industrial and Islamic-finance institutions** (but not its race-religion entitlement clauses). Pakistan is the warning: religion-based nationhood already failed the Bengal delta. **(Strong on economics and Pakistan; Moderate elsewhere)**

**5. Bengal.** Bengal has real civilizational depth (Pala, Bengal Sultanate, Mughal agrarian frontier per Eaton, a unique Bengali-language Islamic literature, Sufi synthesis). The strongest objections: the Bengali half is shared with West Bengal, the Muslim half with the ummah, and 1971 rejected religion as sufficient ground for nationhood. Verdict: Bangladesh is the political core of a **distinct regional Islamicate civilization of the delta**, plural in origin. **(Moderate)**

**6. Critics.** Every critic forces a concession: 1971 and 1952 must be foundational; rights cannot be ranked; the ummah must be honoured but not made sovereign; a material programme for workers is required; minorities' plural origins must be built into the name and law; and identity cannot replace hard power.

**7. Policy.** The panel majority recommends a **Thin-to-Medium** version: preamble recognition of a plural delta heritage shaped decisively by Islam; a civic definition of the nation (resolving the Adivasi grievance); a shared curriculum across general and madrasa streams; a developmental-state economic programme with Islamic finance under strong regulation; balanced Bay-of-Bengal diplomacy; and **stronger** minority safeguards than today. A minority (IT-A) argues only a shari'a-grounded Thick version is honest.

**8. Risks.** The highest risks are majoritarian drift, Islamist outbidding, reopening of 1971, economic neglect and an India crisis. Each has early warning indicators the study circle should monitor publicly.

**Bottom line.** As *history and cultural self-understanding*, a Bengal-delta Islamicate civilizational identity is defensible and can strengthen national confidence. As *legal hierarchy*, it would repeat Pakistan's error and invite the abuses visible in today's declared civilizational states. The framework survives its critics **only** if it is plural in origin, civic in membership, developmental in substance and protective of minorities by design. **(Overall grade: Moderate)**

### (3) Fifteen discussion questions for the study circle

1. Is a "civilization" something a people *inherits* or something it *builds*? What follows for Bangladesh if Bennabi is right that civilisation is work, not heritage?
2. Lucian Pye meant "a civilization pretending to be a state" as a criticism. Why did it become a compliment? Who benefits from the shift?
3. Which of our seven criteria (Phase 2) is most important, and should "inclusive capacity" (C6) be part of the definition at all?
4. Does Qur'an 49:13 ("peoples and tribes that you may know one another") support nationalism, limit it, or neither?
5. Madani's composite nationalism was written for Muslims as a minority. What does it demand of Muslims as a 91% majority?
6. Iqbal called the territorial homeland "the biggest of the new idols" yet helped create a Muslim homeland. Is that a contradiction or a strategy?
7. If Hallaq is right that the modern state cannot be Islamic, where should Islamic civilization live: in the state, in society, or both?
8. Eaton argues Islam spread in Bengal through forest clearing and rice cultivation. Does an indigenous, agrarian Islam strengthen or weaken today's calls for scriptural purity?
9. Can a Bangladeshi civilizational identity claim Tagore, Jibanananda and the Charyapada? What is lost if it cannot?
10. Was 1971 a rejection of religion-based nationhood, of Pakistani domination, or both? What evidence would settle the question?
11. What should Bangladesh learn from Indonesia's Pancasila, and what should it avoid from Malaysia's Article 153 and Article 160?
12. Has any "civilizational state" protected its minorities better than an ordinary constitutional democracy? If not, why should ours be different?
13. How would the Chakma, Santal or Garo citizen describe the "civilization of the delta"? Should the framework's name change after listening to them?
14. What is the economic content of civilizational nationalism, if any, beyond mobilisation? Design one measurable 10-year target.
15. Which three early-warning signs in the Phase 8 risk register should this study circle monitor and publish each year, and how?
